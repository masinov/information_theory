\begin{minipage}{24pc}
  \small
  \noindent{\scshape abstract.}\enspace
  For a thing to appear in any respect, it must bear a difference, and something must be as it is
  in virtue of that difference. This paper proposes these two conditions, differentiation and
  receptive dependence, as the minimal core of perception and of whatever knowledge rests on it,
  since nothing is perceived that does not appear. It states them between actual terms only, without
  alternatives, order, representation, or a knowing subject, and it proposes registration, a
  condition's holding in virtue of a difference, as the elementary form of appearing. What a subject
  adds lies outside the core.
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Theories of knowledge ordinarily begin with a subject who believes, judges, or is justified, and ask
under what conditions such states amount to knowledge. This paper asks what must hold before that:
what is required for anything to appear at all.

Its answer has two parts. There must be a difference in what is under consideration, since nothing
appears where everything is alike. And something must be as it is in virtue of that difference. The
paper calls this structure \emph{registration}, and proposes it as the elementary form of appearing.

To appear is to show up. The word is not used in the sense of seeming, as opposed to being, and it
implies no subject to whom something appears. Perception and knowledge, which involve a subject, are
not treated here; nothing is perceived that does not appear, so on this account of appearing the
conditions are necessary for them. The conditions are formal: they are stated without reference to space, time, matter, or
causation, and examples from particular domains show how they are met there without fixing what
they are.

The exposition uses ordinary logical resources---identity, distinction, relation, and
inference---to state its commitments. This does not attribute those resources to what the
commitments describe, and in particular not to a knowing subject. A distinction drawn in the
exposition is not thereby a distinction drawn by its object.

The proposal has close neighbours. \textcite{dretske1981} analyses the information carried by a
signal in terms of nomic dependence and conditional probability, and separates it from meaning and
belief. Floridi's diaphoric definition of data takes a lack of uniformity---a difference---as the
most elementary constituent of information \autocite{floridi2011}, and his informational structural
realism holds that the relation of difference is constitutive of its relata \autocite{floridi2008}.
Smith's metaphysics of presence makes \emph{registration} its central notion, as the way in which a
world comes to be taken as one of objects \autocite{smith1996}.\footnote{The present use of the term
is narrower. Smith's registration is performed by subjects and yields objects; registration here
presupposes neither.} Enactivist accounts describe cognition as the structural coupling of a system
with a medium, both of which belong to a larger organization \autocite{maturana1987,thompson2007}.
The present account differs from these in three respects. What is depended on is always a
difference between actual terms. The dependence is stated without probabilities, causes, or
alternatives. And its direction is distinguished from any order of occurrences.

Sections~\ref{sec:diff} and~\ref{sec:art} concern the difference in what is under consideration.
Sections~\ref{sec:dep} and~\ref{sec:recv} concern the dependence and the receiving side.
Section~\ref{sec:reg} defines registration, Section~\ref{sec:real} gives two cases, and
Section~\ref{sec:order} concerns order.

\section{Differentiation}\label{sec:diff}

Let \F{} designate what is under consideration. Calling it a \emph{field} fixes the scope of the
inquiry; it implies neither a spatial region, nor a collection of discrete objects, nor something
already presented to a subject. Nor does the notation establish a boundary intrinsic to reality:
\F{} identifies what the account concerns, and any claim about its actual boundaries would require
separate justification.

\begin{thesis}[D]{Differentiation}
  For anything of \F{} to appear, \F{} must bear a difference.
\end{thesis}

The account takes appearing to be contrastive: nothing appears where everything is alike, and what
appears stands out from something. If \F{} is taken whole, it appears by its difference from
something beside it, and what is under consideration is then the two together. If \F{} is
everything, there is nothing beside it, and it appears only through the differences within it.

The notion of a difference is taken as primitive. It is not defined, since any definition would
already use it: to define something is to set it apart from something else. It can be pointed to.
Take anything that is not alike throughout. Something in it is one way, and something in it is
another way: there are two, and in some respect they are not alike. The account calls this a
\emph{contrast}. The two that differ are its \emph{terms}, that in which they differ is its
\emph{respect}, and what has the two terms \emph{bears} the contrast. A contrast is always in some
respect. The account deals with contrasts between two terms; where more terms differ, it considers
them two at a time. Two contrasts are the same when their terms and their respect are the same, so
the same two terms may bear two contrasts, in two respects.

Both terms of a contrast are actual. The account uses no difference between something actual and an
alternative, that is, a way that nothing actual is. This is a commitment of the account. To use
alternatives it would have to say where they are held, in a stock of possibilities, in a repertoire
belonging to whatever receives, or in the description that specifies them, or else take on a modal
commitment about the thing itself. It takes on none of these. The core therefore does not
provide for three things to appear: which way something is as against a way that nothing is;
anything of a term that differs from nothing actual; and that two terms are alike. \enquote{Actual}
is meant without tense.

That \F{} bears a contrast says that there is a difference, not that \F{} could be differentiated.
It says nothing of which contrasts \F{} bears, of whether one differentiation of \F{} is privileged,
or of whether what differs is objects, qualities, regions, or values. It implies nothing about
change or order. And it involves no knowing subject: that \F{} bears a contrast does not imply that
anything differentiates it.

The contrasts concerned are those of \F{} itself. The account concerns a differentiation
attributable to its target, rather than one supplied entirely by an unrelated description. A
difference that holds, even in part, because the account has specified it belongs to the
description, and it is left out. A respect that the account chooses is not thereby one that it
supplies: what matters is whether the terms differ in it apart from the choosing.

\section{Articulation}\label{sec:art}

An account speaks of a particular contrast of \F{} by means of an \emph{articulation}, written
\[
  \langle a,\delta,b\rangle_F ,
\]
which names two terms, $a$ and $b$, and a respect, $\delta$. The articulation is \emph{specified} by
the account, not by \F. It is \emph{realized} when $a$ and $b$ are actual terms of \F{} and differ in
$\delta$. Specification selects a contrast; it does not create one, and it can fail: a name may pick
out nothing, or the terms may not differ in the respect named. The order in which the terms are
written, their labels, and their grouping belong to the articulation, not to \F. Specification
fixes what an inquiry concerns, as \F{} fixes its scope; it does not supply what the inquiry finds.

The terms of a realized articulation are both actual. They are not alternatives of which one is the
case and the other not; the contrast is the difference between two terms that are both actual. The
schema does not assume that $a$ and $b$ exist before the contrast as independent
objects.\footnote{That a difference need not be grounded in terms identified before it is argued by
Deleuze in his critique of the subordination of difference to identity
\autocite[ch.~1]{deleuze1994}; the present account leaves the question open. Deleuze also rejects
the possible as a mere image of the real, and the present account likewise uses no stock of
possibilities.}

Two misreadings of the schema should be excluded. First, $\delta$ is not a third item situated
between $a$ and $b$. To treat it as a separator would presuppose spatial organization, whereas
$\delta$ is the respect in which the two differ. Second, $b$ need not comprise everything that
remains once $a$ has been selected. That reading would require an exhaustive partition of \F, and a
contrast between two terms is not equivalent to a division into \enquote{this} and
\enquote{everything else within the field}: the latter adds the requirement that every case within
the scope fall on one side or the other (Figure~\ref{fig:contrast}).

    \draw[rel] (a) to[bend left=55] node[above] {$\delta$} (b);
    \node[panel] at (1.1,-1.35) {(a) contrast};

    \end{scope}
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \caption{Two constructions over \F. (a)~An articulation $\langle a,\delta,b\rangle_F$: $a$ and $b$
    mark terms, both actual, and $\delta$, drawn as a relation rather than as an item between them,

\end{figure}

A realized articulation remains an articulation of \F. Nothing so far implies that its contrast
appears anywhere.

\section{Receptive Dependence}\label{sec:dep}

That \F{} bears a contrast, and that something else is in some condition, are two facts, and
nothing said so far connects them. They could be unrelated. A further commitment is therefore
needed.

\begin{thesis}[C]{Receptive dependence}
  Something of \F{} appears only where some condition of something holds, at least partly, in
  virtue of a contrast of \F.
\end{thesis}

A \emph{condition} is something's being a certain way. (C) contains (D), since it requires a
contrast of \F; what it adds is the dependence.

\enquote{In virtue of} is not defined. It is the notion in use whenever one thing is given as the
reason why another is so. Two things may both be the case without either having anything to do with
the other; or one may be the case because the other is. (C) says that appearing is of the second
kind. It is receptive: where something of \F{} appears, something is as it is because of how \F{}
is. The account also calls this \emph{dependence}. In what follows $K$ is a contrast of \F{} and $q$
a condition that holds in virtue of it.

The notion is used under three conditions. It relates what holds: $K$ and $q$ both hold. Nothing
holds in virtue of itself. And it has a direction: that $q$ holds in virtue of $K$ does not bring
with it that $K$ holds in virtue of $q$.\footnote{The first and third features are
familiar from the literature on metaphysical ground \autocite{fine2012}. The account does not
identify the dependence with ground.}

The dependence is on the difference itself, not merely on how one of its terms is. A condition that
held in virtue of how $a$ is alone would not depend on the contrast of $a$ and $b$. This does not
set the contrast against how the two terms are together: the contrast is the respect in which what
they jointly are matters to the condition.

Dependence is neither resemblance nor pairing. Something elsewhere may be like the contrast, and its
parts may be paired with the terms of the contrast, without holding in virtue of it
(Figure~\ref{fig:correspondence}).\footnote{\textcite[ch.~1]{goodman1976} makes the parallel point
about representation: resemblance is reflexive and symmetric, whereas representation is neither, so
resemblance cannot be what representation consists in.} Both relations can be read in either
direction, and neither ties one thing to this contrast rather than to any that is like it. Nor does
agreement show dependence: where two things agree because both depend on a third, their agreement
does not establish that either depends on the other. (C) requires none of these, and in particular
no pairing.

\begin{figure}[tp]
  \centering\small
  \begin{tikzpicture}
    \foreach \x/\lab/\links in {0/{(a) resemblance}/0, 5.4/{(b) pairing}/1} {
      \begin{scope}[xshift=\x cm]
        \node[side] at (0,1.25) {one field};
        \node[side] at (2.4,1.25) {another field};
        \node[term, label=above:$a$]  (a) at (0,0.5)  {};
        \node[term, label=below:$b$]  (b) at (0,-0.5) {};
        \node[term, label=above:$c$]  (r) at (2.4,0.5)  {};
        \node[term, label=below:$d$] (s) at (2.4,-0.5) {};
        \draw[rel] (a) to[bend right=60] node[left] {$\delta$} (b);
        \draw[rel] (r) to[bend left=60] (s);
        \ifnum\links=1
          \draw[corr] (a) -- (r);
          \draw[corr] (b) -- (s);
        \fi
        \node[panel] at (1.2,-1.55) {\lab};
      \end{scope}
    }
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \caption{Two relations that are not dependence. (a)~Resemblance: two contrasts are alike.
    (b)~Pairing: $a$ is paired with $c$, and $b$ with $d$. The pairing is drawn without direction,
    since it can be read either way.}
  \label{fig:correspondence}
\end{figure}

Nor is dependence the same as what would have held otherwise. $q$ may hold in virtue of $K$
although, had $K$ not held, something else would have brought $q$ about; and it can be that neither
$K$ nor $q$ holds without the other although both depend on a third. What would have held otherwise
is evidence of dependence, not what it consists in.\footnote{Counterfactual analyses of dependence
require alternatives to the actual case. Where contrasts hold necessarily, they meet a further
difficulty: on the standard semantics, counterfactuals with impossible antecedents are all
vacuously true \autocite{lewis1973}; for an alternative semantics, see \textcite{nolan1997}.}

The dependence may be partial: $q$ may hold in virtue of $K$ and of other things as well, among them
how its bearer is.\footnote{This accommodates the enactivist point that a perturbation triggers,
but does not specify, the changes it occasions in a system, which are fixed by the system's own
structure \autocite{maturana1987}.} Bateson's definition of information as \enquote{a difference
which makes a difference} is close to this \autocite{bateson1972}; MacKay had earlier described
information as a distinction that makes a difference \autocite{mackay1969}. The difference is $K$,
and its making a difference is $q$'s holding in virtue of it. The dependence should not be replaced
by an appeal to \enquote{systematic} or \enquote{relevant} coupling, which brings in a standard of
relevance or a regularity across cases.

What the dependence consists in is a matter for each domain: in physical domains it may be causal,
in others logical or constitutive. The core requires that there be such a dependence, not that it be
of one kind. It is admitted, not derived from what it relates: a relation cannot be obtained by
describing what it relates. Whether it is transitive is left open.

A condition may depend on several contrasts. It then stands in the same relation to each, and the
core does not single out one of them.

The direction of the dependence gives two roles. In an instance of it, what bears the contrast is
the \emph{target}, and what bears the dependent condition is the \emph{receiver}. The roles are
places in the relation, not natures of what occupies them: the same thing may be target in one
instance and receiver in another.

\section{The Receiving Side}\label{sec:recv}

Let \R{} designate what bears the dependent condition.\footnote{The vocabulary of reception recalls
Kant's characterization of sensibility as receptivity, the capacity to acquire representations
through being affected by objects \autocite[A19/B33; cf.\ A50/B74]{kant1998}. The receiver
introduced here is thinner: it involves no representations, and nothing is claimed about forms of
intuition.} \R{} need not be a person, a conscious subject, or an independently bounded system, and it
may be an aspect of a larger organization that also includes \F, since a distinction of roles does
not establish two substances.

The receiving side needs one condition, not two. The condition is actual, and it need not be like
the contrast of \F{} in any way; nothing in it is assigned to one term of the contrast rather than
the other. No further contrast borne by \R{} is required for it to register a contrast of \F. In
particular, \R{} need not be in a second condition from which the first differs.

The account attributes no act of distinguishing to \R. That its condition holds in virtue of a
contrast of \F{} is a fact about the contrast and the condition, not something that \R{} has to
note. It is the account that picks them
out.

The condition of \R{} can itself appear only as (D) allows: where it differs from something actual,
and that contrast is depended on in turn.

Neither side comes with the other. A contrast of \F{} may hold while nothing depends on it, and
\R{} may be in conditions that depend on nothing in \F.

This fixes how much individuation the account admits. There must be enough to distinguish the
contrast from the condition that depends on it. There need not be enough to identify enduring
objects or successive states of a continuing subject. To assume that would build into the
foundation a persisting, bounded bearer---which is what an account of a knowing subject must
eventually explain.

\section{Registration}\label{sec:reg}

\begin{thesis}{Definition of registration}
  A contrast $K$ is registered where a condition $q$ of something holds, at least partly, in virtue
  of $K$. What bears $q$ registers $K$.
\end{thesis}

\begin{figure}[tp]
  \centering\small
  \begin{tikzpicture}
    \node[side] at (0,1.45) {target (\F)};
    \node[side] at (5.0,1.45) {receiver (\R)};
    % registered contrast
    \node[term, label=above:$a$] (a) at (0,0.6)  {};
    \node[term, label=below:$b$] (b) at (0,-0.6) {};
    \draw[rel] (a) to[bend left=55] coordinate[pos=.5] (dm) (b);
    \node[anchor=east, inner sep=2pt] at (dm) {$\delta$};
    % the dependent condition
    \node[term, label=below:$q$]  (q) at (5.0,0) {};
    % receptive dependence
    \draw[dep] ([xshift=3pt]dm) -- node[above, pos=.5] {$C$} ([xshift=-4pt]q.west);
    \node[note] at (0,-1.65)   {registered\\[-1pt]contrast};
    \node[note] at (2.5,-1.65) {receptive\\[-1pt]dependence};
    \node[note] at (5.0,-1.65) {dependent\\[-1pt]condition};
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \caption{Registration. A condition $q$ of \R{} holds at least partly in virtue of the contrast of
    $a$ and $b$ in $\delta$. The arrow runs from the contrast, not from either term, and denotes
    receptive dependence only: it represents neither a transfer nor an order of occurrences. The
    columns are roles, not regions.}
  \label{fig:registration}
\end{figure}

Registration is always of a particular contrast: to say that \R{} registers \F{} without
qualification is to leave the claim incomplete. It is an actual relation between a contrast and a
condition that both hold, not a disposition of \R{} to respond (Figure~\ref{fig:registration}).

The account proposes registration as the elementary form of appearing. Every registration is an
appearing. And whatever else appears is claimed to appear through registrations that are part of
it, not merely alongside it. The second claim is not shown here: it states what an account of the
three things set aside in Section~\ref{sec:diff} would have to meet.

The proposal is broad, and deliberately so. A contrast appears wherever it contributes to why some
condition holds. The condition need not display the contrast, distinguish it from others, or keep
anything of it.

Registration is supplied by neither side alone. There is the contrast, the condition, and the
dependence of the second on the first. Keeping them distinct prevents registration from being
attributed to the target merely because the target is differentiated, or to the receiver merely
because it is in some condition.

The definition attributes to the receiver no understanding, identification, or judgement. Where
registration is realized physically, it is found in systems to which nobody would attribute
knowledge, and by itself it does not place anything \enquote{in the logical space of
reasons}.\footnote{The phrase is from \textcite[§\,36]{sellars1956}; \textcite[ch.~4]{brandom1994}
develops the contrast between such reliable differential responsiveness and conceptual capacities.
In perception, Dretske's non-epistemic seeing, in which an object is visually differentiated from
its surroundings without any belief about it, contains a registration in the present sense
\autocite{dretske1969}.} It requires no transfer from \F{} to \R, no resemblance, and no
representational space or repertoire in \R.

What is registered is always a difference between actual terms: that $a$ differs from $b$. Accounts
of information and knowledge that begin from alternatives presuppose a set among which the actual
case is singled out;\footnote{Shannon's theory measures information as selection from a set of
possible messages \autocite{shannon1948}, and Dretske's informational content is defined by
conditional probabilities over such alternatives \autocite{dretske1981}. In epistemology,
relevant-alternatives theories and contrastivism analyse knowledge as the elimination of
alternatives \autocite{dretske1970,lewis1996,schaffer2004}; counterfactual and interventionist
analyses of dependence share the same structure \autocite{lewis1973,woodward2003}.} the core has no
such set.

\section{Two Cases}\label{sec:real}

In the first case the dependence is causal. Let $a$ and $b$ be two bodies of unequal mass, resting
on the pans of a balance, and let the beam be inclined. $K$ is the contrast of the bodies in mass,
and $q$ is the beam's being inclined. $q$ holds in virtue of $K$, in part: the beam is inclined
because the two sides are unequally loaded, and not because of the mass of either body considered
alone. How far it is inclined depends on more: on how much the masses differ, on how the balance is
built, and on where the bodies rest. The balance registers the difference in mass. It has no
repertoire of the masses that the bodies might have had.

In the second the dependence is constitutive. Let $a$ and $b$ be two parts of a whole $W$ that
differ in a respect $\delta$. $K$ is their contrast, and $q$ is the whole's being non-uniform in
$\delta$. $q$ holds in virtue of $K$: $W$ is non-uniform because two of its parts differ. Nothing
causes anything here, and nothing passes from one thing to another. The whole registers a contrast
within itself, through a condition of its own.

Nobody would speak of perception or knowledge in either case: those involve a subject, which the
core does not contain.

\section{Registration and Order}\label{sec:order}

The core assumes no order. The dependence has a direction, but the direction of a relation is not
an ordering of occurrences: that $q$ holds in virtue of $K$ does not place either before the other.

The core therefore cannot say that a receiver \emph{acquires} what it registers, \emph{retains} it,
or \emph{compares} it with something else.\footnote{The three operations closely parallel the
synthesis of apprehension, reproduction, and recognition in the first edition of Kant's
Transcendental Deduction \autocite[A98--A110]{kant1998}, and Husserl's distinction between
retention and recollection \autocite{husserl1991} analyses the second of them. Both treat the
operations as presupposing temporal form; the present account speaks only of order, of which
temporal order is one kind.} Retention requires that one receiver be identified across distinct
places of some ordering. Acquisition requires that a place at which a contrast is not registered
stand in an asymmetric relation to one at which it is. Neither follows from (C).

What they require is at least an asymmetric relation among places, with no requirement that any two
places be comparable, and with no metric, no linearity, and no universal index. Such a relation may
still run in a circle, so it is less than an order in the usual sense, and what more is needed is
left open. Temporal succession is one relation of this kind. Whether a relation that is not
temporal, such as the order of steps in a proof, could serve for acquisition or retention is also
left open.

If receptive dependence is written with a directed symbol, such as an arrow from \F{} to \R, that
symbol should not also serve, without further explanation, to represent order. The direction of
dependence and the asymmetry of an ordering play different roles and should be kept notationally
distinct.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:concl}

Table~\ref{tab:commitments} summarizes the elements of the account.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering\small
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
  \setlength{\tabcolsep}{5pt}
  \captionabove{The elements of the account, with what each permits and what each leaves
                unestablished.}
  \label{tab:commitments}
  \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{8.8em}
                                    >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X
                                    >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X@{}}
    \toprule
    \scshape element & \scshape what it permits & \scshape what it does not establish \\
    \midrule
    (D) Differentiation
      & A difference attributable to what is under consideration, by which something of it can
        appear.
      & Which contrasts \F{} bears; that they appear anywhere. \\
    Actual terms only
      & A core without possibilities or a repertoire.
      & Which way a term is as against an alternative; anything of a lone term; that two terms are
        alike. \\
    Specified articulation
      & Terms and the respect in which they differ.
      & Its own realization, an exhaustive partition, or objecthood. \\
    (C) Receptive \mbox{dependence}
      & Registration of a contrast; the roles of target and receiver.
      & Understanding, representation, order, acquisition, or retention. \\
    The receiver \R
      & A condition that depends on a contrast.
      & A knowing subject; a second condition; any resemblance to the contrast. \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The proposal is that for anything to appear, what is under consideration must bear a difference
between actual terms, and something must be as it is in virtue of that difference, with target and
receiver distinguished as roles in that dependence.

Five questions remain open. What the dependence consists in within a given domain, and whether it
can be explained by more elementary relations; to write it as an arrow would not answer this. What
must be added to a registration for something to appear to a subject, that is, to be perceived. How
which way a term is, and that two terms are alike, can appear without alternatives. What acquisition
and retention require beyond an asymmetric relation. And whether what is said of things that cannot
act on whoever speaks of them, things picked out by description, future events, and mathematical
objects, rests on registration at all.

The account ends at registration. Order, memory, recognition, and representation have not been
presupposed in reaching it, and none of them has yet been derived from it.
