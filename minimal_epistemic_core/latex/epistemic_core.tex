\begin{minipage}{24pc}
  \small
  \noindent{\scshape abstract.}\enspace
  This paper states a minimal core of conditions that hold wherever something is taken up, that
  is, perceived in the widest sense, whatever the domain. The core is not itself an epistemic
  relation. It is a structure that every taking-up contains, and it is found in much that nobody
  would call epistemic; what makes a taking-up epistemic is no part of it. The account starts from
  two commitments and one decision. The first commitment is that what is taken up is something in
  particular, and is taken up as it differs from something else. The second is that the taking-up
  holds, at least partly, in virtue of what is taken up. The decision is that the account uses only
  differences between actual terms, never differences from alternatives; its reasons and its cost
  are stated. On this basis registration is defined: one contrast between actual terms holds at
  least partly in virtue of another. The definition presupposes neither understanding, nor
  representation, nor order, nor a pairing of terms, nor a knowing subject, nor any repertoire of
  possibilities. The account begins from three notions that it does not define but makes clear by
  pointing to them: being taken up, a difference, and one thing's holding in virtue of another. The conditions are formal: they presuppose no spatial, temporal, or physical structure.
  They are claimed to be necessary for any taking-up, not sufficient, and the claim is no stronger
  than the two commitments. Two cases, one causal and one not, show that the structure is met by
  dependences of different kinds. The paper lists everything it assumes, by kind, and what the core
  does not provide.
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Theories of knowledge ordinarily begin with a subject who believes, judges, or is justified, and ask
under what conditions such states amount to knowledge. This paper addresses a prior question: what
must hold for anything to be taken up at all, that is, perceived in the widest sense. Its answer is a set of necessary conditions, not a sufficient account. It identifies what
any theory of perception presupposes, and what theories of belief and knowledge presuppose so far as
belief and knowledge involve something's being taken up; it leaves to such theories what makes a
relation epistemic. The assessment of judgement, in particular, lies outside its scope.

\emph{Taken up} means perceived, in the widest sense of the word. Its clear case is perception by
the senses: something is seen, or heard, or felt. The phrase is used in place of \enquote{perceived}
only so as not to limit the subject to the senses, or to creatures that have them. The notion is not
defined. It is the ordinary notion of what the account is about, and perception by the senses shows what
is meant. The account says what every case of it contains; it does not say what makes something a case
of it (Section~\ref{sec:real}). The word \emph{registration} is reserved for the structure defined in
Section~\ref{sec:reg}. The claim of the paper is that every taking-up contains a registration. The
converse is false: the structure is found where nothing is taken up by anything
(Section~\ref{sec:real}). The core is therefore not an epistemic relation of a simple kind. It is
what every taking-up contains.

Not every epistemic relation is covered. A claim may concern something by description, a future
event, or a mathematical object. Whether such relations involve something's being taken up is left
open (Section~\ref{sec:concl}).

The conditions are formal. They are stated without reference to space, time, matter, or causation,
and they are meant to hold whatever the domain in which something is taken up. Examples drawn from
particular domains show how the conditions are met there; they do not fix what the conditions are.

The account is a reconstruction, in this sense: each element is introduced only at the point where
the account cannot proceed without it, and each is accompanied by a statement of what it does not
establish. Every item is given its kind: commitment, decision, notion that is not defined,
condition of method, stipulation, assumption, consequence, or presupposition. One caution applies throughout. The
exposition necessarily employs ordinary logical resources---identity, distinction, relation, and
inference---but using them to state the account does not attribute them to what the account
describes, and in particular not to a knowing subject. A distinction drawn in the exposition is not
thereby a distinction drawn by its object. In the same way, the exposition uses modal words to say
what the conditions leave open; this attributes no possibilities to what it describes.

The proposal has close neighbours. \textcite{dretske1981} analyses the information carried by a
signal in terms of nomic dependence and conditional probability, and separates it from meaning and
belief. Floridi's diaphoric definition of data takes a lack of uniformity---a difference---as the
most elementary constituent of information \autocite{floridi2011}, and his informational structural
realism holds that the relation of difference is constitutive of its relata \autocite{floridi2008}.
Smith's metaphysics of presence makes \emph{registration} its central notion, as the way in which a
world comes to be taken as one of objects \autocite{smith1996}.\footnote{The present use of the term
is narrower. Smith's registration is performed by subjects and yields objects; registration here
presupposes neither and concerns only the dependence of one contrast on another.} Enactivist
accounts describe cognition as the structural coupling of a system with a medium, both of which
belong to a larger organization \autocite{maturana1987,thompson2007}. The present account differs
from each in three respects. It separates the contrast that is registered, the contrast that
registers it, and the dependence that connects them. It states that dependence between contrasts of
actual terms, without probabilities, causes, or alternatives on either side, so that it applies
beyond the physical domain and requires no repertoire of possibilities. And it distinguishes the
direction of dependence from any order of occurrences, leaving open what kind of order further
epistemic operations require.

Section~\ref{sec:prem} states the two commitments and the decision, in ordinary words.
Sections~\ref{sec:diff} to~\ref{sec:recv} introduce the vocabulary in which they are made exact:
fields and contrasts, articulations, the dependence of one contrast on another, and the receiving
field. Section~\ref{sec:reg} defines registration and shows how every taking-up contains one.
Section~\ref{sec:real} gives two cases, and Section~\ref{sec:order} considers order, which the
structure does not assume. Section~\ref{sec:concl} lists everything the account assumes, what it rules out and what
it does not include, what it leaves undone, and the questions it leaves open.

\section{Two Commitments and a Decision}\label{sec:prem}

The account starts from two commitments about what it is for something to be taken up, and from one
decision about what the account itself will use.

\begin{thesis}[D]{Differentiation}
  What is taken up is something in particular, and it is taken up as it differs, in some respect,
  from something else.
\end{thesis}

The commitment has two parts. The first is that a taking-up is of something in particular: there is
a fact about what is taken up. This is assumed. The second is that the thing is taken up as it
differs. The reason for it lies in what \enquote{in particular} requires. For a thing to be some way
in particular, there must be a way that it is not, and its being this way as against another lies in
that difference. What is not one way as against any other is no way in particular, and cannot be
what a taking-up is of. A taking-up of something as the particular thing it is, is then a taking-up
of it as it differs. This is a reason, not a proof: it supposes that to take up something in
particular is to take it up in what makes it particular.

Two things are not said. It is not said that a thing is nothing but its differences. Whether a
thing's character consists in how it differs, or is more than that, is left open; the commitment
concerns the particularity of its way, not what the thing is. Nor is it said, in (D), that what a
thing differs from must be actual. That is the matter of the decision below.

(D) can be refused in two ways: by holding that a taking-up need not be of anything in particular,
or by holding that something can be taken up as the particular thing it is without its difference
from anything being taken up.

\begin{thesis}[C]{Reception}
  The taking-up holds, at least partly, in virtue of what is taken up.
\end{thesis}

This commitment says which relation the account is about: the one in which something receives. It
is not a hypothesis that some case of taking-up might overturn, since a relation in which nothing
held in virtue of what it is said to be of would not be a taking-up. Its content can be seen from
the relations that it sets aside, which are examined in Section~\ref{sec:dep}: two things may be
alike, or correspond term for term, or agree because both hold in virtue of a third, without either
holding in virtue of the other. \enquote{In virtue of} is one of the notions that the account begins from without defining;
Section~\ref{sec:dep} says what is meant by it. Since, by (D),
what is taken up is something as it differs, (C) says that the taking-up holds at least partly in
virtue of a difference.

(C) can be refused by holding that something can be taken up although nothing holds in virtue of
it. The account regards that as a change of subject.

\begin{thesis}{Decision: actual terms only}
  The account uses only differences between terms that are both actual. It uses no difference
  between something actual and an alternative, that is, a way that nothing actual is.
\end{thesis}

This is a decision about what the account will use, not a result. \enquote{Actual} is not defined either: it means what is the case, as opposed to an alternative
that is not. It is not tensed: nothing
is said about when. The decision has three reasons, one for each place where alternatives might be
brought in.

The first place is in what is taken up. (D) and (C) do not by themselves exclude that a thing is
taken up as it differs from a way that nothing actual is. But such a way has to be supplied from
somewhere. It may be supplied by a stock of possibilities, which is an assumption about what there
is that the account does not make. It may be supplied by what receives, which is the third place
below. Or it may be supplied by the account's own specification of it, and then the difference is
one that holds partly because the account has specified it, which the condition of method excludes
(Section~\ref{sec:diff}). With an actual other term nothing has to be supplied: both terms are
actual. This reason can be refused by admitting a stock of possibilities.

The second place is in the dependence. One might say that a taking-up holds in virtue of what is
taken up when it would not have held had that been otherwise. But the two come apart. One thing may
hold in virtue of another although, had the second not held, a third would have brought the first
about. And it can be that neither of two things holds without the other although neither holds in
virtue of the other, because both hold in virtue of a third. What would have held otherwise is
therefore evidence of dependence, often the best there is, and in this simple form it is not what
dependence consists in. More refined analyses that also use alternatives are not examined here; the
account does not state the dependence over alternatives.

The third place is in what receives. Alternatives might be held there as a repertoire, against which
the actual case is singled out. The structure defined in Section~\ref{sec:reg} uses no repertoire.
A repertoire would also bring in a relation of another kind: its members would have to stand for
what is not the case, and to stand for what is not the case is not to hold in virtue of it. Whether
some kinds of taking-up require a repertoire is not settled here. If they do, it enters as a
further commitment, stated as such.

The decision applies to both sides. What is taken up is treated as a difference between actual
terms, and so is that by which it is taken up (Section~\ref{sec:recv}).

The decision has a cost. The core does not provide for taking up which way something is as against
an alternative; anything of a term that differs from nothing actual in the respect concerned; or
that two terms do not differ; and a receiver with one condition only is not covered
(Section~\ref{sec:recv}). Nothing is claimed about whether these occur. If they do, they need
what the core does not contain.

\emph{What kind of claim this is.} The two commitments are an analysis of what it is for something
to be taken up as the particular thing it is. If the analysis is right, the conditions that follow
hold of every taking-up, of whatever kind, and nothing in them concerns what sort of thing receives. The necessity claimed is of that kind and no stronger: it is the necessity of the
analysis, and it holds given the decision. It is not claimed that anything is ever taken up, nor
that the conditions hold of anything apart from a taking-up. It may be objected that reasoning of
this form shows only how we must think of epistemic relations, not how they are
\autocite{stroud1968}. The commitments do not pass from how we think to how things are. They say
what a relation must be in order to be a taking-up of something, and they leave open whether,
where, and by what anything is taken up. What remains true in the objection is that an analysis is
answerable to the notion analysed, and that we know that notion from our own case. That is why the
ways of refusing each commitment are stated.

\enquote{In particular} does not mean \enquote{one only}. The core does not single out one difference
(Section~\ref{sec:dep}).

\section{Fields and Contrasts}\label{sec:diff}

The account begins from the notion of a difference. It is not defined, because any definition of it
would use it: to define anything is to set it apart from something else. It can only be pointed to.
Take anything that is not alike throughout. Something in it is one way, and something in it is
another way: there are two, and in some respect they are not alike. That is a difference, and the
account calls it a \emph{contrast}. The two that differ are its \emph{terms}, and that in which they
differ is its \emph{respect}. Whatever has the two terms is said to \emph{bear} the contrast.

Three conditions state how the notion is used here, so that a use that goes against it can be shown.
A contrast is always in some respect. It is between two terms, and no term differs from itself. And,
by the decision of Section~\ref{sec:prem}, both terms are actual.

Two remarks are added, which are not conditions. The account does not say what respects there are,
nor whether bare distinctness is one; if it is, any two things bear a contrast, and (D) asks
correspondingly little. And a contrast does not divide what bears it exhaustively: its two terms
need not be all there is to what bears it.

Whatever bears a contrast is, insofar as it does, a \emph{field}. To be a field is thus an aspect,
not a kind of thing: the term implies neither a spatial region, nor a collection of discrete
objects, nor something already presented to a subject. What it is for terms to be the terms of one
field is not said, and the account does not say how fields are individuated. Let \F{} designate
the field under consideration. The designation fixes the scope of the inquiry. It does not
establish a boundary intrinsic to reality: \F{} identifies what the account concerns, and any claim
about its actual boundaries would require separate justification.

By stipulation, two contrasts are the same when they have the same terms and the same respect. The
same two terms may therefore bear two contrasts, in two respects.

That \F{} bears a contrast is not a modal claim. It does not say that \F{} can be differentiated, or
that it admits differentiations which it does not have; it says that there is a difference. Nothing
is thereby said about which contrasts \F{} bears, about whether some differentiation of \F{} is
uniquely privileged, or about whether what differs would be objects, qualities, regions, values, or
something else. Nor is anything implied about change or about any order. Possibilities and
capacities of a field are not thereby denied. They are not part of the core, and if a later stage
of the theory needs them, they must enter as commitments of their own, stated as such.

The account is subject to one condition of method.

\begin{thesis}{Scope of the account}
  The account concerns only contrasts that a field bears, and none that holds, even partly, because
  the account has specified it.
\end{thesis}

The reason is that the account concerns a differentiation attributable to its target, rather than
one supplied entirely by an unrelated description; a contrast is attributable to a field when the
field bears it. This is a necessary condition for the route of explanation pursued here, and it
agrees with (C): a difference that held only because of a description could be taken up only as a
difference in the description. The account does not argue against the view that all differentiation
is supplied by descriptions. It states what that view would cost: if it held, no contrast would be
attributable to a target, and the account would have nothing to articulate. No knowing subject is
involved: that \F{} bears a contrast does not imply that anything differentiates it.

\section{Articulation}\label{sec:art}

An account speaks of a particular contrast by means of an \emph{articulation}, written
\[
  \langle a,\delta,b\rangle_F ,
\]
which names two terms, $a$ and $b$, and a \emph{respect}, $\delta$. An articulation is
\emph{specified} when its terms and respect are fixed, and it is the account, not \F, that specifies
it. It is \emph{realized} when the terms it names are actual terms of \F{} and differ in the respect
$\delta$. The \emph{contrast} of a realized articulation is the difference of $a$ and $b$ in that
respect; two articulations may share a respect and have different contrasts. An articulation can
fail to be realized in two ways: one of its names may pick out nothing, or the terms it names may
not differ in the respect named.

The terms of a realized articulation are both actual. They are not alternatives of which one is the
case and the other not, and the contrast is not a choice between them; it is the difference between
two terms that are both actual. The schema has two terms because a contrast is between two terms; a
differentiation among more terms is a number of contrasts. The schema does not assume that $a$ and
$b$ exist prior to the contrast as independent objects, and it does not deny it.\footnote{That a
difference need not be grounded in terms identified before it is argued by Deleuze in his critique
of the subordination of difference to identity \autocite[ch.~1]{deleuze1994}; the present account
leaves the question open. Deleuze also rejects the possible as a mere image of the real, and the
present account likewise uses no stock of possibilities.}

Two misreadings of the schema should be excluded. First, $\delta$ is not a third item situated
between $a$ and $b$. To treat it as a separator would presuppose spatial organization, whereas
$\delta$ is the respect in which the two differ. Second, $b$ need not comprise everything that
remains once $a$ has been selected. That reading would require an exhaustive partition of \F, and a
contrast between two terms is not equivalent to a division into \enquote{this} and
\enquote{everything else within the field}: the latter adds the requirement that every case within
the scope fall on one side or the other. Figure~\ref{fig:contrast} sets the two constructions side
by side.

    \draw[rel] (a) to[bend left=55] node[above] {$\delta$} (b);

  \label{fig:contrast}

Specification chooses the respect and selects a contrast. By the condition of
Section~\ref{sec:diff}, it does not create the contrast. Whether \F{} realizes the specified
articulation, and whether anything holds in virtue of its contrast, are not settled by the
specification, which can therefore fail. Specification fixes what an inquiry concerns, as \F{} fixes
its scope; it does not supply what the inquiry finds.

The same condition leaves out any contrast that holds, even partly, because of the specification or
its notation: because of the order, the labels, or the grouping of the terms. Suppose that $a$ and
$b$ differ in the respect $\delta$, and let $\delta^{*}$ be the respect: $\delta$, together with
being written first. That $a$ and $b$ differ in $\delta^{*}$ holds partly because of how the account
has written them. It is left out for that reason, and not because something other than \F{} has a
part in it: a contrast of \F{} may well hold partly in virtue of a contrast borne elsewhere
(Section~\ref{sec:dep}). A differentiation of this mixed kind is not left out wholesale. The
contrast in $\delta$ is within the scope of the account; the remainder is not.

Two things follow from the definitions. First, a field in which an articulation is realized bears
that articulation's contrast. This establishes neither which contrasts a field bears, nor that any
field bears a contrast apart from a taking-up, nor any modal claim about \F. Second, what differs
from no actual other term in a given respect is a term of no contrast in that respect, and the core
provides for taking up nothing of it in that respect.

That a field bears contrasts does not fix which articulation an account will specify. An account of
any particular case must therefore specify the articulation it concerns, and it may fail to pick out
anything. And a realized articulation is an articulation of \F: nothing so far implies that its
contrast is taken up by anything.

\section{Receptive Dependence}\label{sec:dep}

That \F{} bears a contrast, and that some other contrast is borne somewhere, leaves the two
unconnected. Commitment (C) requires a connection: that the one hold in virtue of the other.
This is another of the notions that the account begins from, and like the notion of a difference
it is not defined but pointed to. Two things may both be the case without either having anything to do with the other. Or
one may be the case because the other is: the second accounts for the first, and the first is so on
account of it. Whoever asks why something is so, and is given something else that is so as the
answer, is using the notion. The account calls it holding \emph{in virtue of}. It also speaks of
one contrast \emph{depending} on another, and of \emph{receptive dependence}; these are the same
notion. Where it says that something holds because of something that is not a contrast, the same
ordinary notion is meant, but the conditions below are not claimed for that case.

The notion is not defined in other terms, for the following reason. The analyses available either
state the
dependence over alternatives to the actual case, as counterfactual analyses do, which the account
has decided not to do (Section~\ref{sec:prem}); or belong to one domain, as causal analyses do; or
identify it with metaphysical ground. The account does none of these. From the literature on ground
it takes two features only: that dependence of this kind has a direction, and that it relates what
actually holds \autocite{fine2012}.\footnote{Counterfactual analyses of dependence require
alternatives to the actual case. Where contrasts hold necessarily, they meet a further difficulty:
on the standard semantics, counterfactuals with impossible antecedents are all vacuously true
\autocite{lewis1973}; for an alternative semantics, see \textcite{nolan1997}.}

Three conditions state how the notion is used here. First, it relates contrasts that both hold. A statement of
dependence is therefore not a statement about what would hold otherwise. Second, nothing holds in
virtue of itself. This is stipulated, so that no contrast depends on itself. Third, the
dependence has a direction: that $K'$ holds in virtue of $K$ is one thing, that $K$ holds in virtue
of $K'$ is another, and the first does not bring the second with it. The conditions are stated for
the case in which both things related are contrasts, which is the case that the definition of
registration uses. (C), as stated in Section~\ref{sec:prem}, relates a taking-up to what is taken
up; Section~\ref{sec:recv} says how the account brings that under a relation between two contrasts.

Three further matters are permitted or left open; they are not conditions. The dependence may be
partial: $K'$ may hold in virtue of $K$ and of other things as well, which may lie in the field that
bears $K'$.\footnote{This accommodates the enactivist point that a perturbation triggers, but does
not specify, the changes it occasions in a system, which are fixed by the system's own structure
\autocite{maturana1987}.} Whether two distinct contrasts can each hold partly in virtue of the other
is left open. (C) does not exclude it: it requires that the taking-up depend on the difference, not
that the difference be independent of the taking-up. A view on which a difference in \F{} is partly
made in being taken up is described, in these terms, as two contrasts each depending on the other.
Whether the dependence is transitive is also left open. The two questions are connected. If the
dependence is transitive, two contrasts that depended on each other would each depend on itself,
which the second condition forbids. So transitivity excludes mutual dependence, and mutual
dependence excludes transitivity. The account does not decide between them. If the dependence is
transitive, it is a strict partial order among contrasts; if it is not, loops through a third
contrast are not excluded.

Dependence is to be distinguished from three relations that can hold where neither contrast
holds in virtue of the other. Two contrasts may resemble one another.\footnote{\textcite[ch.~1]{goodman1976} makes the
parallel point about representation: resemblance is reflexive and symmetric, whereas representation
is neither, so resemblance cannot be what representation consists in.} The terms of one may be
paired with the terms of the other, each with each (Figure~\ref{fig:correspondence}). And two
contrasts may agree because both hold in virtue of a third. In the first two cases the relation can
be read in either direction; resemblance holds equally between a contrast and every other that is
like it, and a pairing can be set up between any two contrasts. In the third, whatever connection there is lies in the dependence of each on the third.
None of these is what (C) requires, and (C) requires none of them. In particular it requires no
pairing of terms: it relates one contrast to another, and which term of the one answers to which
term of the other is not part of it. Where a pairing is needed, it must enter as a further
commitment, stated as such.

The dependence so bound is close to what Bateson's definition of information as \enquote{a
difference which makes a difference} expresses \autocite{bateson1972}; MacKay had earlier described information as a
distinction that makes a difference \autocite{mackay1969}. The phrase compresses what the present
account keeps apart: the first difference is $K$, the second is $K'$, and the making is the
dependence. The dependence should not be replaced by talk of \enquote{systematic} or
\enquote{relevant} coupling. Such terms bring in a standard of relevance, or a regularity across
cases, which the account has not supplied.

Whether $K'$ holds in virtue of $K$ need not be fixed by a description of the two contrasts. In some
kinds of case it is fixed; the second case of Section~\ref{sec:real} is one. In others it is not:
two pairs of contrasts alike in every description, and in any pairing of their terms, may differ in
that in one pair the second contrast holds in virtue of the first and in the other pair it does
not. Whether, in cases of that kind, the dependence in one instance holds because of other actual
instances, as in a regularity, is left open.

The dependence does not single out one contrast. If $K'$ holds partly in virtue of $K$ and partly in
virtue of another contrast $K^{*}$, it depends on both; and if the dependence is transitive, it
depends on whatever $K$ depends on. In the sense of registration defined in Section~\ref{sec:reg},
a contrast registers every contrast it depends on. Which of these a taking-up is of is not settled
by the core.

The direction of the dependence defines two roles. Where $K'$ holds in virtue of $K$, the field
that bears $K$ is the \emph{target} in that instance, and the field that bears $K'$ is the
\emph{receiver}. A field has a role only through the contrast it bears in that instance: one field may bear
both contrasts, and is then target and receiver at once. Roles are thus places in a relation, not natures of what
occupies them, and the same field may be a target in one instance and a receiver in another.

Two limits of what has been said should be noted. The dependence is not derived from the existence
of contrasts: a relation cannot be obtained merely by describing what it relates. It is what (C)
commits the account to. And the account does not say what the dependence consists in within a particular
domain. That is a question for that domain: in physical domains it may be causal, in others logical or
constitutive. The core is in this sense a schema. It states the conditions that a dependence
satisfies, and each domain supplies the dependence.

\section{The Receiving Field}\label{sec:recv}

(C) speaks of the taking-up as holding in virtue of what is taken up. This section says what the
account takes the taking-up to be.\footnote{The vocabulary of reception recalls Kant's
characterization of sensibility as receptivity, the capacity to acquire representations through
being affected by objects \autocite[A19/B33; cf.\ A50/B74]{kant1998}. The receiver introduced here
is thinner: it involves no representations, and nothing is claimed about forms of intuition.}

The taking-up is some way that what receives is. The account assumes that it is some way in
particular. By the reasoning given for (D), its being that way in particular lies in its differing
from another way; and by the decision of Section~\ref{sec:prem}, the account treats that other way
as actual too. The account therefore takes the taking-up to be one term of a contrast between two
actual \emph{conditions} of what receives, written $r$ and $r'$, and it calls that contrast the
receiving contrast, $K'$. The other condition, $r'$, need not hold in virtue of anything in the
target. It is only that from which $r$ differs.

This is the least secure step of the account, and it is marked as such. Nothing in the two
commitments requires that what receives have two actual conditions. That it does is the decision
for actual terms, applied to the receiving side as to the target; a receiver with one condition
only, differing from nothing actual, is not covered. And one further assumption is used, which is
not argued: that a contrast holds at least partly in virtue of whatever one of its terms is what it
is in virtue of. With it, $K'$ holds at least partly in virtue of what is taken up.

\begin{thesis}[R]{The receiving contrast and its bearer}
  Where a contrast $K'$ holds in virtue of a contrast $K$, $K'$ is distinct from $K$. What bears
  $K'$ is, insofar as it does, a field; \R{} designates it, and it may be the field that bears $K$.
\end{thesis}

(R) is not a further commitment. That $K'$ is distinct from $K$ follows from the stipulation that
nothing holds in virtue of itself, and that its bearer is a field follows from what a field is. \F{}
and \R{} are letters for the two roles in the instance considered; nothing else is meant by them.

\R{} is a field in exactly the sense in which \F{} is one: whatever bears a contrast, insofar as it
does. Bearing a contrast is not a property peculiar to receivers. What holds of the target therefore
holds of \R: the contrast concerned is attributable to the field that bears it, and it is the
account that picks it out. \R{} itself distinguishes nothing. In particular, it does not distinguish
a taking-up from its absence: that $K'$ holds in virtue of $K$ is a fact about the two contrasts,
not something that \R{} notes.

\R{} is not a person, a conscious subject, or an independently bounded system. Nothing follows about
whether \R{} and \F{} are distinct fields, parts of a larger one, or a single field bearing two
contrasts, since a distinction of contrasts does not entail a distinction of substances. A field
may be target and receiver at once, but only through two contrasts.

As with the terms of a realized articulation, $r$ and $r'$ are both actual: they are not
alternatives between which \R{} is poised. Nothing requires that $K'$ be like $K$ in any respect,
and no condition is assigned to any particular term of $K$.

Neither contrast is given with the other. Nothing in what a contrast of \F{} is implies that some
contrast holds in virtue of it, and a field may bear contrasts that hold in virtue of nothing in
\F. A field's merely differing within itself does not make it a receiver of anything in \F.

This fixes how much individuation the account admits. There must be enough to distinguish $K$ from
$K'$, and to take $r$ and $r'$ as terms of one contrast. There need not be enough to identify
enduring objects or successive states of a continuing subject. To treat these stronger forms of
individuation as already established would build into the foundation a persisting, bounded
bearer---which is precisely what an account of a knowing subject must eventually explain.

\section{Registration}\label{sec:reg}

Registration can now be defined, in a restricted, structural sense.

\begin{thesis}{Definition of registration}
  A contrast $K$ is registered by a contrast $K'$ when $K'$ holds at least partly in virtue of $K$.
  $K$ is then the \emph{registered} contrast and $K'$ the \emph{registering} contrast, and the
  field that bears $K'$ is said to register $K$.
\end{thesis}

Registration is always of a particular contrast: to say that \R{} registers \F{} without
qualification is to leave the claim incomplete. An account that states a registration must
therefore specify an articulation; the specification is the account's, and it is no part of the
registration. Registration is an actual relation between contrasts that hold, not a standing
disposition of \R{} to respond. Figure~\ref{fig:registration} depicts the structure so defined.

The claim of the paper can now be stated exactly: every taking-up contains a registration. By (D),
what is taken up is something as it differs from something else, and by the decision that is a
contrast $K$ between actual terms. By (C), the taking-up holds at least partly in virtue of $K$. By
Section~\ref{sec:recv}, the account takes the taking-up to be one term of a contrast $K'$ between
actual conditions of what receives, and $K'$ then holds at least partly in virtue of $K$. So $K$ is
registered by $K'$, and the receiving contrast of Section~\ref{sec:recv} is the registering
contrast. The three steps differ in kind. The first uses a commitment and the decision.
The second uses a commitment. The third uses the decision, applied to the receiving side, and two
assumptions that are not argued: that the taking-up is some way in particular, and that a contrast
holds partly in virtue of whatever one of its terms is what it is in virtue of.

The converse does not hold. A registration is not, as such, a taking-up of anything, and
Section~\ref{sec:real} gives a case. The core says nothing about what must be added to a
registration for something to be taken up.

The definition attributes to the receiver no understanding, identification, or judgement, since none
of these capacities has been introduced. Where registration is realized physically, it is found in
systems to which nobody would attribute knowledge, and by itself it does not place anything
\enquote{in the logical space of reasons}.\footnote{The phrase is from \textcite[§\,36]{sellars1956};
\textcite[ch.~4]{brandom1994} develops the contrast between such reliable differential
responsiveness and conceptual capacities. In perception, Dretske's non-epistemic seeing, in which an
object is visually differentiated from its surroundings without any belief about it, is a case that
contains a registration in the present sense \autocite{dretske1969}.} The definition does not
require that anything pass from \F{} to \R, nor that the registering contrast be like the
registered one in any respect. Nor does it require a representational space, or any repertoire of
alternatives, in \R. Registration relates contrasts that hold, and nothing in \R{} need stand for
conditions that \R{} does not have.

Registration so defined is supplied by neither field in isolation. It comprises three distinct
contributions: from the target, the registered contrast; from the receiver, the contrast between its
conditions; and between them, the dependence that connects the two. Keeping these contributions
distinct prevents registration from being attributed to the target merely because the target is
differentiated, or to the receiver merely because its conditions differ. This does not explain the
emergence of a knowing subject, but it identifies what such an explanation would have to elaborate.

What is registered is always a difference between actual terms. The core therefore does not provide
for registering which way a term is, as against a way that nothing actual is; anything of a term
that differs from nothing actual in the respect concerned; or that two terms do not differ in a
respect, since where there is no contrast there is nothing to register. Accounts of information and
knowledge that start from alternatives presuppose a set among which the actual case is singled
out.\footnote{Shannon's theory measures information as selection from a set of possible messages
\autocite{shannon1948}, and Dretske's informational content is defined by conditional probabilities
over such alternatives \autocite{dretske1981}. In epistemology, relevant-alternatives theories and
contrastivism analyse knowledge as the elimination of alternatives
\autocite{dretske1970,lewis1996,schaffer2004}; counterfactual and interventionist analyses of
dependence share the same structure \autocite{lewis1973,woodward2003}.} The core uses no such set,
by the decision of Section~\ref{sec:prem}. How the three things just listed come about, where they
do, is left to a later stage. Registering which way a term is appears to need at least a pairing of
terms with conditions, which registration does not include.

\section{Two Cases}\label{sec:real}

The core is a schema, and a schema should be shown to have instances. Two are given here, chosen so
that the dependence is of a different kind in each. They test the schema; they do not fix what it
says, and no argument in the preceding sections relies on them.

\emph{A causal case.} Let $a$ and $b$ be two bodies that differ in mass, resting on the two pans of
a balance that is at rest, with its ends at different heights. $K$ is the contrast of $a$ and $b$ in
mass; $K'$ is the contrast of the two ends in height. Both hold, and all four terms are actual. $K'$
holds at least partly in virtue of $K$, and the dependence is causal. It is partial: $K'$ holds also
because of how the balance is built. $K$ does not hold in virtue of $K'$. The beam has no repertoire
of the weights that the bodies might have had. The case shows that registration does not single out
one contrast: $K'$ holds partly in virtue of the difference in where the two bodies rest on the
beam, and so registers that contrast as well. Something passes from the bodies to the beam in this
case, and the dependence came about through a process in time. The core neither requires nor
excludes either.

\emph{A constitutive case.} Let $\delta$ be a fine respect and $\Delta$ a coarser one, such that
terms which differ in $\delta$ in a certain way differ, for that reason, in $\Delta$; a finer and a
coarser classification of the same terms are an example. Let $a$ and $b$ be actual terms that
differ in $\delta$ in that way. $K$ is their contrast in $\delta$, and $K'$ is their contrast in
$\Delta$. They are two contrasts, since the respects differ, although the terms are the same. $K'$
holds in virtue of $K$, and $K$ does not hold in virtue of $K'$. The dependence is not causal, and
nothing in it is temporal or spatial. Three things differ from the first case. $K'$ holds in virtue
of $K$ alone, given the two respects, so the schema does not require that what receives contribute
anything. Whether $K'$ depends on $K$ is fixed by a description of the two. And one field bears
both contrasts: it registers one of its own contrasts through a second.

Three things follow. First, the schema is met by dependences of two kinds. Second, in each case the
dependence is identified by the means of its own domain. Those means may include the comparison of
other cases, or of what would happen otherwise; the conditions of the core say what the dependence
relates when it holds, not how it is found to hold. Third, registration is not itself epistemic. In
the second case nobody would say that anything is taken up, and the schema is met. Wherever one
difference holds in virtue of another there is a registration, so registrations are very common.
This is consistent with a statement of necessary conditions, and it fixes how the result is to be
read. The core states what every taking-up contains, and much else contains the same. What must be
added for something to be taken up is not said by the core.

\section{Registration and Order}\label{sec:order}

The core assumes no order of any kind. Its contrasts are not sequences. It does not say that the
conditions of \R{} are successive, and it does not say that they are not: \enquote{actual} is not
tensed, and whether what is earlier and what is later are both actual is not settled here. The
dependence has a direction, but the direction of a relation is not an ordering of occurrences: that
$K'$ holds in virtue of $K$ does not place either before the other.

The core therefore cannot describe a receiver as \emph{acquiring} a contrast, \emph{retaining} it,
or \emph{comparing} it with another. What each of these appears to require is stated here for the
use of later stages; it is not derived.\footnote{The three operations closely parallel the synthesis
of apprehension, reproduction, and recognition in the first edition of Kant's Transcendental
Deduction \autocite[A98--A110]{kant1998}, and Husserl's distinction between retention and
recollection \autocite{husserl1991} analyses the second of them. Both treat the operations as
presupposing temporal form; the present account speaks only of order, of which temporal order is one
kind.} Comparison appears to require two registering contrasts that both hold, and no order.
Retention appears to require that one receiver be identified across distinct places of some
ordering. Acquisition appears to require that a place of such an ordering at which a contrast is
not registered stand in an asymmetric relation to one at which it is. Neither retention nor
acquisition follows from the definition of registration.

What they appear to require is order in the loosest sense: an asymmetric relation among places, with
no requirement that any two places be comparable, and with no metric, no linearity, and no universal
index. Whether this relation must also be transitive, and so form a strict partial order, may be
left open. Temporal succession is one way of realizing such an order; the order of steps in an
inference or a proof is another, which is not temporal. The core is compatible with either and
requires neither. If the dependence of Section~\ref{sec:dep} is transitive, it is itself an order of
this kind among contrasts; whether it can serve here is not examined.

Since the direction of a relation is not an ordering of occurrences, if receptive dependence is
written with a directed symbol, such as an arrow from
\F{} to \R, that symbol should not also serve, without further explanation, to represent order. The
direction of dependence and the asymmetry of an ordering play different roles in the account and
should be kept notationally distinct.

The result is that every taking-up, in whatever domain, contains a registration: one contrast
between actual terms holding at least partly in virtue of another, with target and receiver
distinguished as roles within that dependence. The conditions are necessary for a taking-up and not
sufficient. Their necessity is that of the analysis given in the two commitments, and it holds
given the decision. Nothing is claimed about relations to things by description.

The core is minimal, but it is not empty. It rules out one thing: that a taking-up consists in
resemblance, in a pairing of terms, or in an agreement that two contrasts owe to a third, since in
none of these does one contrast hold in virtue of the other (Section~\ref{sec:dep}). It leaves out
of the account, as a condition of method, any contrast that holds even partly because of the
account's own specification (Sections~\ref{sec:diff} and~\ref{sec:art}). And it does not include,
among the conditions of registration, any of the following: that something pass from target to
receiver; a repertoire of alternatives; time, or order of any kind; a pairing of terms with
conditions; a knowing subject, concepts, or representations. That these are not among the conditions
does not show that a taking-up can occur without them in a given domain, and it does not deny that
they are present in particular epistemic relations. What is claimed is only that the two commitments
do not require them: wherever they are needed, they must enter as further commitments, stated as
such.

What the account leaves undone should be stated as plainly. First, the account does
not say what respects there are, or how fields are individuated. Second, the core does not say what
must be added to a registration for something to be taken up; registrations are very common, and
most are not epistemic. Third, by the decision for actual terms the core does not provide for taking
up which way something is as against an alternative, anything of a term that differs from nothing
actual in the respect concerned, or that two terms do not differ; and a receiver with one condition
only is not covered. Fourth, the treatment of the receiving side (Section~\ref{sec:recv}) rests on
that decision and on two assumptions that are not argued. Fifth, registration does not single out
one contrast, and since transitivity is left open, the core does not say whether a registering
contrast depends on more than what it immediately depends on. Sixth, the core says by what
conditions receptive dependence is bound, not how an instance of it is known to be one, and it sets
no bound on what a receiving field contributes. Seventh, \enquote{actual} is not tensed. Whether
what is earlier and what is later are both actual, and so whether there can be a contrast between
them, is not settled here and belongs with the question of order. Eighth, the account picks out the
contrasts of its targets by description, and neither commitment covers a relation to something by
description: the account uses a relation to its targets that it does not itself cover.

Five questions remain open. The first is what the dependence consists in, and what gives it its
direction, in a given domain. The second is whether it can be explained in terms of more elementary
relations, and whether it is transitive or can be mutual; to write it as an arrow would not answer
this. The third is what must be added to a registration for something to be taken up, and how taking
up which way a term is, anything of a lone term, and likeness come about. The fourth is what order
acquisition and retention require, whether anything stronger than an asymmetric relation is needed,
and whether the dependence itself, if it is transitive, supplies that order. The fifth is whether
relations to something by description, to the future, and to mathematical objects involve
something's being taken up. The account ends at registration. Order, memory, recognition, and
representation have not been presupposed in reaching it, and none of them has yet been derived from
it.
