  \small
  \noindent{\scshape abstract.}\enspace
  It is often suggested that no decision theory can be optimal on every fair decision problem,
  because the standards by which theories are judged conflict. This paper makes the claim precise
  and determines when it holds. On a class of finite problems with prediction, in which a structural
  representation fixes every causal counterfactual and fairness and well-definedness are separate
  conditions, a decision theory is any assignment of policies to problems. Ex-ante optimality is
  then always attainable, so the claim is false as stated. Within a single problem, every
  inconsistency among ex-ante optimality, structural causal dominance, ratifiability, commitment
  stability, and an evidential criterion has a core of one or two criteria; the conflict between
  ex-ante optimality and dominance is possible exactly when the state law depends on the prescribed
  action, and it survives imperfect prediction and randomisation. Across problems, each problem is
  reduced to its causal matrix, whose rows are the belief states induced by the agent's policies and
  whose diagonal is ex-ante value. With common affine invariance the cross-problem axioms again
  reduce to singleton and pairwise cores, one of which is Briggs's theorem. With invariance under row
  translations, neutrality and contraction force a rule to choose every option, on exactly the
  domains satisfying a reachability condition; and principal binary independence, with symmetry,
  ordering, and monotonicity, characterises ex-ante evaluation. Both results depend on how a smaller
  menu is modelled: if the belief model is held fixed, baseline-invariant evaluation is menu coherent
  and contraction no longer forces ex-ante evaluation. The impossibilities concern the preservation
  of comparisons when the belief perspectives of removed options disappear, not the preservation of
  choices when options disappear.
\end{minipage}
