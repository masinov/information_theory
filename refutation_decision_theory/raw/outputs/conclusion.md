## 20. Philosophical discussion

The preceding sections establish theorems about stated definitions. This section interprets them. Its claims are arguments, not results, and they are marked where they go beyond what has been proved. Its aim is the one that motivated the whole inquiry: to say exactly where the disagreement among decision theories is located, so that the apparent paradoxes can be separated from the substantive question they conceal.

### 20.1. What a formalisation decides before any theorem is proved

The first lesson concerns method, and the paper is its own evidence. Part I compared decision theories on problems that supply, for every policy π, the law μ_π of the world's response (Definition 2.2). On that class, ex-ante optimality is attainable by the most naive construction available, choosing a maximiser problem by problem (Theorem 3.6), and every within-problem impossibility reduces to a single problem on which two acceptable sets are disjoint (Lemma 3.8). The result is cheap for a reason Section 14 makes explicit: causal, evidential and functional decision theory disagree precisely about where μ_π comes from, and a model that supplies μ_π has already settled their dispute. The fairness condition of Definition 2.4 did the same for problems that refer to a theory: it excluded them by stipulation, and Remark 3.7 then found the corresponding construction ill defined, a verdict reversed once problems became programs (Theorem 15.6).

The general point is this. To state a decision problem formally, one must say how the agent's alternatives are to be evaluated, and that choice is usually the one in dispute. A formal result about decision theories is therefore only as informative as the specification of alternatives it presupposes, and the specification is often made in the problem format rather than in the theorem, where it is easy to miss. Appendix A and Section 19.3 exist to prevent this. Reading the literature with the same question in mind, namely *which part of the answer was built into the format?*, removes much of the appearance that one theory has been refuted by a problem rather than by an assumption.

### 20.2. The locus of the conflict

The theories compared here agree about more than their dispute suggests. They share the payoffs, the probabilities of the world's own randomness, and the rule of maximising expected value. Section 2.3 isolates what remains in a single subscript. The causal value of an alternative a at h averages with μ_π, the law induced by the policy actually adopted. Its ex-ante value averages with μ_{π[h←a]}, the law the alternative itself would induce. Every within-problem conflict in Part I is a consequence of that difference, and Theorem 4.2 states exactly when it can arise: when, and only when, the law of the states preceding a choice depends on what the agent prescribes. Proposition 6.5 gives the converse. If the world's response does not depend on the agent's policy, the ex-ante optimum is causally optimal and no conflict exists.

The same choice reappears in each part of the paper in a different form.

- **Part I:** it is the choice of law under which an alternative is evaluated.
- **Part II:** it is the choice between the row and the diagonal of the causal matrix, and the cross-problem results depend on whether the belief perspectives associated with removed options disappear with them (Section 10).
- **Part III:** oracle substitution recovers the diagonal and port-only intervention recovers a row (Proposition 14.4). At the axiomatic level, the choice becomes one between two notions of when computations are the same: grounded equivalence, which identifies components by their behaviour once the actual agent is inserted, and parametric equivalence, which identifies them only if they agree under every substitution (Definition 18.5). Calibration together with grounded equivalence forces causal evaluation (Lemma 18.9); parametric equivalence admits ex-ante evaluation satisfying every desideratum (Corollary 18.13).

This, then, is the locus of the conflict. The theories do not disagree about how to choose among specified alternatives. They disagree about what the alternatives to the agent's own choice are. Causal decision theory evaluates an alternative in a world that keeps the actual past, including whatever in it predicted the agent, and varies only the act: Lewis's small miracle, located at the moment of choice. Functional decision theory evaluates an alternative in a world where the agent's decision procedure outputs something else, so that everything computing that procedure varies with it. Neither world is actual, and nothing in the actual course of events selects one of them. The remark following Proposition 15.9 states this in its simplest form: a fixed program computing 0 and a linked call to an agent that outputs 0 agree on the actual execution and differ under replacement. Theorem 18.10 states it in general: no total semantics can both transport a computation through alternative outputs and identify every presentation that has the same actual behaviour, because actual behaviour discards the information transport needs.

The philosophical literature on counterpossibles meets the same obstruction from the other side (Section 19.2). Williamson's argument for vacuism identifies antecedents by their possible-worlds intension, which is empty for every impossibility; grounded extensionality identifies components by their instantiated behaviour, which is the same for the identity context and the constant context at the baseline. In both cases an identification appropriate to what actually happens collapses the distinctions that non-vacuous counterfactuals need. Wolpert and Benford (2013) reached a related diagnosis of Newcomb's problem: the two classic arguments presuppose incompatible formalisations of how choice and prediction are related. The present results locate that incompatibility precisely and show that it recurs, in the same form, wherever the agent's alternatives must be specified.

### 20.3. Not arbitrary: the coupling between agent and predictor

If the facts of the actual execution do not decide between the two evaluations, one might conclude that the choice is a convention, and that the dispute between causal and functional decision theory is verbal. The results do not support that conclusion, for two reasons.

First, the choice has consequences. Agents built on the two evaluations, placed before the same reliable predictor, obtain different payoffs (Proposition 4.1). A dispute whose resolution changes what agents receive is not merely verbal.

Second, the candidate ways of making the choice are constrained. Section 17 shows that the natural rules for recognising the agent in a closed program each fail in a characteristic way:

- literal matching misses encoded copies;
- local matching manufactures dependence from accidental agreement (Theorem 17.2);
- global matching misses transformed dependence such as 1 − T (Theorem 17.4) and can make optimality decide the halting problem (Theorem 17.5);
- implication-based reasoning about the actual agent yields spurious values (Theorem 17.7) or none (Proposition 17.9).

A semantics is not something one may simply adopt; it must survive these failures.

What, then, could fix the choice? The proposal of this section, which goes beyond anything proved above, is that it is fixed, where it is fixed at all, by the mechanism through which the predictor's output depends on the agent. The actual execution does not reveal that mechanism, but the mechanism is a feature of the world, and in some cases it is known.

- **A predictor that runs the agent's own code** on the agent's own input, through what Section 14 calls a designated call, depends on the agent's decision function in the parametric sense. Changing the function's output changes the prediction, and oracle substitution, hence ex-ante evaluation, describes the situation literally (Theorem 15.5).
- **A predictor whose output is fixed by data that do not compute the agent's function**, such as base rates in a population or a record made after the fact, does not depend on that function at all. Port-only intervention describes it correctly, and the two evaluations coincide whenever the induced laws do (Proposition 6.5).
- **The difficult cases lie between,** and they are the ones that thought experiments typically present. A predictor that scans the agent's brain beforehand reads a physical state that will later produce the decision. Whether its output depends on the agent's decision function, or only on a physical state with which the decision is correlated, depends on whether the agent is identified with the computation the brain implements. That identification is not a fact about the prediction mechanism alone; it is a commitment about what an agent is.

On this view, the debate's error has been to seek a decision rule that is correct independently of the coupling between agent and world. No such rule should be expected. What can be asked of a theory is a correct evaluation given the coupling, and both causal and ex-ante evaluation supply one, for different couplings. The irreducibly philosophical residue is then smaller and better defined than the debate suggests. It is not "which decision theory is rational?", but whether, in the cases where the coupling runs through an agent's physical constitution, the agent is that constitution or the function it computes. The extraction problem left open in Section 20 (whether a dependency graph can be recovered from an arbitrary closed program) is the formal shadow of this question, and Corollary 18.13 shows that once it is answered nothing further stands in the way of a coherent semantics.

### 20.4. When evaluation happens: the Bomb and updatelessness

The Bomb is the case most often offered against functional decision theory, and the paper's treatment of it shows the same locus at work. Proposition 4.4 shows that the problem as usually stated is not well defined: a predictor whose forecast determines the observation, and whose target is the agent's eventual action, cannot be accurate for every policy. Under the policy (L, R), which takes Left after seeing a bomb and Right otherwise, every forecast is wrong. Under the policy (R, L), every forecast is right: a forecast of Right produces a bomb and the agent takes Right, while a forecast of Left produces no bomb and the agent takes Left. The problem therefore leaves undetermined what such an agent receives. The repaired version (Proposition 4.5) fixes the predictor's target as the agent's prescription on seeing a bomb, and under that stipulation the ex-ante optimum is (L, L) while causal evaluation selects (R, L). The case's force thus depends on an assumption the informal statement does not make.

Even repaired, the disagreement concerns the vantage point of evaluation, not the value of outcomes. Proposition 4.5 records both readings. Taken as the policy problem, evaluated before the prediction, the Bomb favours taking Left: the risk of death is 10⁻²⁴ against a near-certain cost of 1. Taken as the already-conditioned problem ("a bomb is visibly present"), ex-ante evaluation itself selects Right, since the options are then worth −M and −1. Functional decision theory evaluates from before the prediction and declines to update on what it sees; this is what *updatelessness* means. The objection to it is that the problem posed is the second one, so that a calculation conducted from the first answers a different question. Counterfactual mugging (Section 4.5) separates the same two vantage points with a prediction about an information set that is unreached on half the paths.

The policy vantage point is not unreasonable. The literature on resolute choice and dynamic consistency (McClennen 1990; Gauthier 1994) and the planning-stage analysis of the absent-minded driver (Piccione and Rubinstein 1997; Aumann et al. 1997) defend executing an ex-ante plan even where it is not optimal ex post, and a causal decision theorist offered the chance to commit before the prediction would commit to Left (Proposition 5.2). The ordinary acceptance of small risks of death, as in daily travel, makes the policy calculation look sensible. The analogy fails, however, at exactly the point in dispute: in ordinary risk the bad outcome is never observed at the moment of choice, whereas in the Bomb the agent chooses after the improbable event has occurred and is in view. Whether the agent's choice now *is* the output the predictor read earlier, so that choosing Right now amounts to having been an agent for whom bombs are placed, is the coupling question of Section 20.3 in temporal form. If it is, the policy vantage is correct and the bomb is the price of a good policy. If it is not, the agent simply stands before a bomb, and the policy calculation describes a choice that is not being made. The Bomb is therefore neither a refutation of functional decision theory nor a mere appeal to emotion; it is a test of whether one accepts the identification on which that theory rests.

### 20.5. Fair and unfair problems

Functional decision theory is claimed to do well on fair problems, those in which the outcome depends only on the agent's behaviour, and problems built against a particular theory are set aside as unfair. The paper's results bear on this restriction in three ways.

First, unfairness is a matter of evaluation semantics, not of the problem's actual behaviour. The fixed-copy diagonal of Theorem 15.6 defeats every globally total computable agent when the embedded copy is held fixed; if the copy is declared linked, the payoff becomes 1[A(d) ≠ A(d)] = 0 and every agent is optimal (the discussion following Proposition 15.9). Since the two declarations agree on every actual execution, the boundary between fair and unfair problems cannot be drawn from behaviour. It is the linked–fixed distinction of Section 20.2 again. The fairness criterion of the functional decision theory literature presupposes a classification it does not supply, and defences that remove counterexamples by declaring them unfair, or by declaring them outside the theory's intended domain, do the work that Definition 2.4 did by stipulation in Part I.

Second, the obstacle to an optimal theory is not choosing well but being computable by the environments. An optimal decision function exists on every admissible class, fixed copies of arbitrary programs included (Theorem 15.3). What fails is an optimal agent that halts on every input, because any such agent can be run by an environment that rewards the opposite (Theorem 15.6), and the oracles that compute an optimal agent are exactly the PA degrees (Theorem 16.3). Optimal choice is always possible problem by problem; no decision procedure that the world can run is optimal everywhere. This vindicates the intuition that it is always easy to construct a counterexample to any predefined theory, and it locates that intuition precisely: the counterexample needs the theory to be a program the environment can execute.

Third, that intuition should not be overstated. Problems built against a theory exist for every theory the environments can compute, but not only for those: a non-computable decision function can also be predictably wrong (Proposition 16.8). What protects an optimal function is optimality, not non-computability. The slogan "unfair problems exist exactly for computable theories" is false; the defensible statement is that global simulability by the environment guarantees a constructible defeat.

### 20.6. Thought experiments as instruments

Idealisation is not the difficulty with these thought experiments. Physics proceeds through frictionless planes, and a reliable predictor is a legitimate device for isolating one feature of a decision, its predictability. The difficulty is that the device is usually specified in exactly the respect that matters least and left unspecified in the respect that matters most. A problem states the predictor's accuracy, sometimes to twenty-four decimal places, and says nothing about its mechanism, its target, or how its errors arise. Yet the results above show that these determine the verdicts:

- **The mechanism** fixes whether the prediction is linked to the agent (Sections 20.2–20.3).
- **The target** decides whether the Bomb is even well defined (Proposition 4.4).
- **The error kernel** is a stipulation (Appendix A, item 8). "Wrong once in 10²⁴ cases" is an overall rate. Whether errors are independent of the agent's policy, concentrated in particular situations, or correlated with the agent's disposition changes the ex-ante calculation, and no statement of the problem fixes it.

Two further habits compound the underspecification. Extreme magnitudes such as an error rate of 10⁻²⁴ and a death cost of 10⁹ push intuitions into a range where they have no evidential basis. And informal arguments import structure the problem does not contain: repeated play, where the problem is one-shot; or, in the other direction, the stipulation that the agent knows it is not inside the predictor's simulation, which the problem may not license. Section 19.3 lists the assumptions any optimality theorem for an agent reading descriptions must state. The informal debate would benefit from the corresponding discipline: no verdict on a predictor problem should be accepted until the predictor's mechanism, target and error kernel are fixed, because until they are, the problem has no determinate answer to dispute.

### 20.7. What the results leave untouched: the utility presupposition

Every result in this paper assumes that outcomes have real-valued payoffs (Definition 2.1). Causal, evidential and functional decision theory share this assumption, and the critique it invites applies to all of them equally.

In the strict sense required by the representation theorems of von Neumann and Morgenstern (1944) and Savage (1954), an agent has a utility function when a single, menu-independent ranking of outcomes, complete, transitive and continuous, accounts for its choices in every situation. Agents with incomparable or lexicographic values fail this. Consider a forced choice between two children, the predicament of Styron's *Sophie's Choice* (Styron 1979), modelled as a coalition of two sub-agents each protecting one child at any price. A small bonus attached to either option does not tip the choice, so the options are incomparable, not tied, and a lexicographic preference has no real-valued representation at all. If the coalition responds to deadlock by refusing, it chooses the outcome both sub-agents rank last. The menu-dependence such an agent exhibits violates exactly the contraction condition of Part II (Sen's α): an option chosen from a larger menu is not chosen from a smaller one that contains it. Harsanyi's aggregation theorem (Harsanyi 1955) adds that if the coalition and its members were all expected-utility maximisers and the coalition respected their unanimous preferences, its utility would be a weighted sum of theirs, so it would trade one commitment against the other, which is what such an agent refuses to do.

There is a weaker sense in which every consistent agent maximises something: indexing outcomes by the situation in which they occur, or redescribing them finely enough, always yields a function that the agent's choices maximise (Broome 1991). That sense is always available and therefore says nothing. It is the counterpart, for utilities, of the lookup tables of Theorem 3.6. Enriching outcomes with factors such as guilt or responsibility can restore a strict representation, and sometimes rightly, since the obvious ranking of outcomes may not be the agent's actual one. But each such enrichment must make predictions across situations, or it becomes the vacuous representation again.

Two consequences follow for the scope of the paper. Its results are conditional on agents with well-defined payoffs, and they do not show that real agents have them. And the epistemic objection to applying decision theory, that an agent's utilities are opaque, shaped by culture and unavailable to introspection, is distinct from the metaphysical one, that for some agents there is no utility function to be known. The first limits application; the second limits the theory's subject matter.

### 20.8. Practical scope

Where the world's response does not depend on the agent's policy, all the evaluations considered here coincide (Proposition 6.5). That covers most practical decisions, and in it the shared core, expected-value reasoning under uncertainty, has a long record of use. The theories' distinctive content is confined to cases of policy dependence, and those are the cases in which each fails some criterion: causal evaluation cannot always be ratified, ex-ante evaluation acts against what the agent observes, and every computable evaluation can be diagonalised. A theory valuable only in the cases where its rivals disagree, and which fails some reasonable criterion in those same cases, has no demonstrated practical advantage over its rivals. Nothing in this paper suggests otherwise.

The one setting in which the coupling view of Section 20.3 promises more is that of agents whose code can be inspected or run by others. There the mechanism of prediction can be known, the dependence can literally be a designated call, and Theorem 15.5 gives a computable optimiser for it. Even there, Theorem 15.6 applies whenever the environment can run the agent with its output held fixed. For human agents facing hypothetical predictors of unspecified mechanism, the dispute between causal and functional decision theory has, on the view defended here, no determinate answer, because the information that would decide it has not been given.

### 20.9. Summary

The disagreement between causal and functional decision theory is not a disagreement about how to choose. It is a disagreement about what the alternatives to one's own choice are. Formally, it is the choice between the row and the diagonal of the causal matrix (Proposition 14.4); semantically, between grounded and parametric equivalence of computations (Definition 18.5). The actual course of events does not settle that choice (Theorem 18.10), and it is not arbitrary (Section 17). This section has proposed that it is settled, where it is settled at all, by the mechanism coupling the agent to whatever depends on it, with an irreducible residue concerning whether an agent is identified with the computation it implements. Where a thought experiment leaves that mechanism unspecified, the dispute has no determinate answer, and the appearance of paradox is what that underspecification leaves behind.
