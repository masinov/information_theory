## Annotations: what each item contributes

- **Ramsey 1926/1931; vNM 1944/47; Savage 1954.**
  - Together they give the representation theorems behind subjective expected utility.
  - Savage's framework treats states as act-independent. That assumption is what makes dominance and expected-utility maximisation agree, and Newcomb cases break it.
- **Jeffrey 1965; Bolker 1966/1967.**
  - Jeffrey gives an evidential, conditional-probability decision theory, in which an act's value is its news value.
  - Bolker's theorem is the representation theorem for Jeffrey's framework. It determines probability and utility only up to fractional linear transformations.
- **Jeffrey 1983 (2nd ed.).** Jeffrey's book-length treatment of ratifiability.
  - SEP credits ratification to Jeffrey [1965] 1983, as a component of evidential decision theory.
  - The idea also appears in Jeffrey 1981, "The Logic of Decision Defended", *Synthese*.
  - Definition: an act is ratifiable when it maximises expected desirability relative to the probabilities the agent would have on finally deciding on it.
  - It is not in the 1965 first edition.
- **Nozick 1969.**
  - The first published statement of Newcomb's problem, which Nozick credits to the physicist William Newcomb.
  - It is the first explicit statement that the dominance principle and the maximise-expected-utility principle can conflict when states are probabilistically, but not causally, dependent on acts.
  - Nozick suggests restricting dominance to cases where the states are probabilistically independent of the acts.
- **Gibbard & Harper 1978.**
  - Distinguishes **U**, causal expected utility computed with probabilities of counterfactuals (Stalnaker conditionals), from **V**, Jeffrey's evidential expected utility.
  - This is the canonical statement of the CDT/EDT split.
  - Introduces **Death in Damascus** in Sect. 11 (SEP confirms the section). Death predicts where you will be, so whichever city you choose makes the other look better. No pure option is stable, which is the founding instability case.
- **Lewis 1979.**
  - The Prisoners' Dilemma, played against a sufficiently similar partner, is two Newcomb problems side by side.
  - This ties Newcomb's problem to game theory.
- **Lewis 1981, "Causal Decision Theory".**
  - Unifies the versions of CDT due to Gibbard–Harper, Skyrms, and Sobel using **dependency hypotheses**: partitions of causal hypotheses that are act-independent.
  - This became the standard formulation.
- **Lewis 1981, "Why Ain'cha Rich?".**
  - Answers the one-boxer's **WAR** argument: "if you're so smart, why aren't you rich?"
  - Lewis's reply: Newcomb's problem rewards irrationality. The predictor pays out for being disposed to one-box, not for choosing rationally.
- **Horgan 1981.** A one-boxing case resting on a backtracking reading of the counterfactuals.
- **Skyrms 1980; Skyrms 1982.**
  - Skyrms's CDT uses an expectation over a partition **K** of act-independent causal factors, together with resiliency.
  - This avoids counterfactual semantics.
- **Eells 1982.**
  - The **tickle defence** of EDT: a self-aware agent's own beliefs and desires screen off the act–state correlation, so EDT also two-boxes.
- **Richter 1984; Weirich 1985.**
  - Early papers on **decision instability**: cases where every option looks worse once you assume you will choose it.
  - In these cases CDT-with-ratifiability gives no verdict.
  - Exact verdicts are from memory; check the texts.
- **Harper 1986.**
  - Proposes maximising causal expected utility among the causally ratifiable options.
  - Allows **mixed strategies** so that a ratifiable option exists in cases like Death in Damascus.
  - The link to Nash equilibria is explicit.
- **Rabinowicz 1988.** Relates ratifiability to stability conditions on deliberation.
- **Skyrms 1990 (book).**
  - **Deliberational dynamics**: credences about one's own act are updated during deliberation.
  - Rest points of this process are deliberational equilibria. In games they connect to Nash and correlated equilibria.
- **Skyrms 1990 (Midwest Studies).** Connects Jeffrey's ratifiability to these deliberational equilibria.
- **Sobel 1994.** Collected essays on causal decision theory, Newcomb's problem, ratifiability and instability.
- **Spohn 1977.**
  - States the principle that **deliberation crowds out prediction**: while deliberating, an agent should not assign subjective probabilities to her own options.
  - Spohn holds that Jeffrey-style act probabilities are therefore illegitimate.
  - This is the main early source for the no-act-probabilities thesis, later echoed by Levi.
- **Price 1986.**
  - An evidentialist critique of CDT, arguing that the agent's deliberative standpoint removes the troubling correlations.
  - That summary is from memory.
- **Joyce 1999.**
  - Gives a representation theorem for CDT, using a Jeffrey/Bolker-style framework with causal (imaging) probabilities.
  - It is the standard foundational treatment.
- **Egan 2007.** Two counterexamples to CDT.
  - **Murder Lesion**: shooting is evidence that you have a lesion that would make you miss.
  - **Psychopath Button**: pressing the button that kills all psychopaths is evidence that you are a psychopath.
  - In both, the intuitively right act (refrain) is not what unconditional CDT recommends. The cases show CDT ignoring evidence that an act provides about its own outcome.
  - SEP notes that versions of CDT with ratification avoid the charge.
- **Arntzenius 2008.**
  - "No regrets": deliberational decision theory that recommends deliberational equilibria, possibly mixed.
  - In Death in Damascus and Egan's cases this gives a stable verdict.
- **Joyce 2012; Joyce 2018.**
  - CDT with deliberation to equilibrium. The agent's credence about her act evolves until no option has a causal-utility advantage.
  - Joyce answers Egan this way and handles instability.
  - 2018 extends the account to "pseudo-Newcomb" problems.
- **Spohn 2012.**
  - Using reflexive decision graphs that model the agent's own decision situation, argues that a properly formulated CDT recommends **one-boxing**.
  - This reverses the standard CDT verdict while keeping the causal approach.
- **Ahmed 2012, "Push the Button".** A case built to show CDT giving the wrong answer.
- **Ahmed 2014 (book).**
  - A book-length defence of EDT: Newcomb cases are rare or ill-posed, while there are realistic cases where CDT goes wrong.
- **Ahmed 2014, "Dicing with Death".**
  - A Death in Damascus variant in which you can pay to toss a coin that Death cannot predict.
  - CDT, as Ahmed argues, does not favour paying for the randomiser even though it guarantees a 50% survival chance, which is a problem for CDT.
  - Details are from memory.
- **Wedgwood 2013.**
  - **Benchmark theory**: each option is assessed relative to a state-dependent benchmark.
  - It is designed to two-box in Newcomb while getting Egan-style cases right.
- **Hare & Hedden 2016.**
  - Classify decisions as **self-reinforcing**, where choosing an option makes it look better, and **self-frustrating**, where choosing an option makes it look worse. Death in Damascus is self-frustrating.
  - They argue that ratificationist and deliberational CDTs give implausible or path-dependent verdicts in such cases.
  - Exact theorems should be checked against the text.
- **Bales 2018.**
  - Analyses the WAR argument and argues it gives CDT no special trouble: comparing agents' wealth does not track rational choice when they face different options or predictions.
- **Spencer & Wells 2019.**
  - A positive argument for two-boxing.
  - They argue the WAR argument fails because it compares agents who face different decision problems.
- **Wells 2019.**
  - Builds a case in which CDT agents predictably end up richer than EDT agents, even though both have the same options, so **equal opportunity** holds.
  - The WAR argument therefore cuts against EDT.
- **Gallow 2020.**
  - A version of CDT on which an act is evaluated by its expected causal improvement, with the act's own evidential import about causal structure taken into account.
  - It is designed to "manage the news" in Egan-style cases.
  - SEP lists it.
- **Weirich, SEP 2008/2024.** The standard survey. It confirms the Death in Damascus source, the ratification attribution, and its reading of Egan.

## Timeline (1969 → present)

- **1926/1931–1954.** Ramsey, then von Neumann & Morgenstern, then Savage. The Savage framework assumes act-independent states.
- **1965–1967.** Jeffrey's *Logic of Decision* (EDT) and Bolker's representation theorem.
- **1969.** Nozick publishes Newcomb's problem: dominance versus expected utility.
- **1977.** Spohn: deliberation crowds out prediction.
- **1978.** Gibbard & Harper: U versus V, the birth of CDT, and Death in Damascus.
- **1979–1982.** Lewis's PD-as-Newcomb, "Causal Decision Theory" and "Why Ain'cha Rich?"; Skyrms's K-partition CDT; Horgan; Eells's tickle defence.
- **1981–1983.** Jeffrey's "Logic of Decision Defended" and 2nd edition: ratifiability.
- **1984–1988.** The instability literature: Richter, Weirich, Harper on mixed strategies and ratifiability, Rabinowicz, Price.
- **1990.** Skyrms: deliberational dynamics and equilibria.
- **1994–1999.** Sobel's essays and Joyce's *Foundations of CDT*. CDT becomes the orthodoxy.
- **2007.** Egan's counterexamples reopen the debate. This is the main turning point.
- **2008–2013.** Arntzenius and Joyce on deliberational equilibria; Spohn reverses to one-boxing CDT; Ahmed's "Push the Button"; Wedgwood's benchmark theory.
- **2014–2018.** Ahmed's book and "Dicing with Death", which revive EDT; Hare & Hedden; Bales; Ahmed's 2018 edited volume.
- **2019–2020.** Spencer & Wells and Wells reverse the WAR argument; Gallow's news-managing CDT.
- **2024.** SEP entry substantively revised.
